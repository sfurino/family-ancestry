Stefano Bonina (born Milazzo, Sicily, 1865) came to Brooklyn in 1900 and, as far as four of five records show, stayed an Italian citizen until he died in 1948 Q1. That makes Papa Petey (born Brooklyn 1909) and Papa (born Brooklyn 1935) Italian citizens by birth, under the old rules and the new.

On Nana's side, Giuseppe Lisuzzo of Lascari was an Italian subject in 1906 and was living back in Lascari in 1924. His son, born in Manhattan in 1901, was admitted home as a US citizen in 1924 and married Santa Cesare in Lascari in 1926. Italy's own papers treat the son as Italian: his birth was entered in the Lascari register in 1912, he served in the Carabinieri from 1920 to 1923, and he came back to America on an Italian passport. His father died in Lascari in 1941, still a Lascari farmer, so on the day Nana was born in New York in 1932 she had a grandfather who was only Italian: she meets the 2025 law's test in her own right Q6.

Under Italy's 2025 law, none of this passes by descent to Sandra, Stephen or Tom, or to any of the grandchildren: their parents and grandparents were born in America, so they were never "exclusively Italian".

It does open the two-years-of-residence route: a foreigner whose parent or grandparent was an Italian citizen by birth can naturalize after two years of legal residence in Italy. That fits the three siblings through their parents, and the grandchildren through Papa and Nana.

The 2025 law is before the EU Court of Justice. If it is reversed (decision expected late 2027 or 2028), both lines qualify by descent all the way down. The records on this page are the start of that file.

The 2025 law, briefly

Until March 2025 Italy recognized citizenship by descent with no limit on generations: you only had to show an unbroken line from an Italian-born ancestor, with nobody in the line having naturalized elsewhere before the next child was born. Decree-law 36/2025, converted into Law 74/2025, added article 3-bis: a person born outside Italy who holds another citizenship is treated as never having been Italian, unless an exception applies. The exception that matters for families like ours: a parent or grandparent held exclusively Italian citizenship on the day the person was born (or on the day that ancestor died, if earlier).

Italy's Constitutional Court upheld the law in March 2026. In July 2026 it asked the EU Court of Justice whether taking citizenship away retroactively is compatible with EU law (case C-816/26). Until that court rules, the law applies in full. Separately, the law cut the residence requirement for naturalization from three years to two for anyone with a parent or grandparent who "is or was a citizen by birth", and since late 2025 descendants of Italians from the United States can get a work visa outside the annual quotas.

The two things a consulate or court asks for are the same under both sets of rules: civil records for every link in the chain (birth, marriage, death), and proof of whether the Italian-born ancestor ever naturalized abroad. That is what the records below are for.

Four records against one, and the one is a daughter-in-law's answer about a man whose own son said the opposite eight years later. But a census answer is not proof either way. Two things would settle it:

  • A USCIS Genealogy index search ($30, about nine months). USCIS holds every naturalization from 1906 on and the alien-registration forms every non-citizen had to file in 1940–41. The answer comes back either as a naturalization file, an alien registration (which would prove he was still an alien in 1940), or nothing.
  • Anything the family kept: his alien registration card (a small card every non-citizen carried after 1940), an Italian passport, or naturalization papers, which would cut the other way and we would want to know.

What hangs on it: whether Papa counts as "Italian by birth" under the 2025 law, which is what the grandchildren's residence route rests on. It does not affect Sandra, Stephen and Tom: their grandfather Papa Petey was born while Stefano was certainly still an alien.

Why this side matters

Under the old rules the line Giuseppe → Giuseppe Jr. → Stella is unbroken: the father was Italian when the son was born, and the son never needed to naturalize because he was born American. Italy itself treated the son as its citizen: it registered his birth, drafted him, and gave him a passport, none of which happens to the son of a naturalized American. Under the 2025 law, Nana meets the test if her grandfather was still only Italian on the day she was born in 1932. He was: he had gone home to Lascari, died there as a farmer in January 1941, and no record anywhere shows him taking another citizenship Q6.

So for the grandchildren there are two separate ways to show "a grandparent who was a citizen by birth": Papa, which depends on the 1940 question Q1, and Nana, which does not. For Sandra, Stephen and Tom, both of their grandfathers, Papa Petey and Giuseppe Jr., were Italian by birth, so their own route holds either way.

Still to find on this side: Giuseppe Sr.'s own birth act (about 1865 to 1867; Lascari's registers for 1864 and 1866 to 1870 have no Giuseppe Lisuzzo, and 1865 is the one year that is online nowhere, so this is a request to the Comune di Lascari) Q7, Maria Stella Cirrito's death (she died before 1941, but not in Lascari's own death registers for 1926 to 1941) Q7, and Nana's New York City birth certificate.

  8. Q8
    What was the "court case with the midwife"?

    Answered by Nana's own account of her father's life, written in 2007 and found on Gianni's CD: at Ellis Island in April 1924 the inspectors thought he was trying to enter on his dead brother's birth certificate (an older brother, also named Giuseppe, had died in infancy). He was held for weeks; a signed and notarized affidavit from the midwife who had delivered him, together with his baptismal certificate, got him admitted, as the "U.S. CIT." stamp on his manifest shows. So it was a Board of Special Inquiry hearing on the island, not a court case; he was born in Manhattan, not Albany; and the paper he carried is on this page.

    What would add to it: Nothing is needed. The hearing file, if it survives in the National Archives' Board of Special Inquiry records, would give the witnesses' names, the midwife's among them.

Giuseppe Lisuzzo Jr.'s birth, entered in the Lascari register in 1912 (certified copy of 1924, with the US visa stamp)

On 7 September 1912 his father, "Lisuzzo Giuseppe fu Antonino", had the New York baptismal certificate (Parrocchia del Preziosissimo Sangue, 115 Baxter Street: born 10 November 1901, baptized December 1901) transcribed into the Lascari civil birth register, after the Italian consulate in New York and the Foreign Ministry in Rome had legalized it. The town hall issued this certified copy on 25 February 1924 "for emigration, for work"; the Pretura di Cefalù legalized it the same day; and the American Consulate in Palermo stamped it: "Italian passport No. 109 was to-day visaed on the strength of this document." So Italy carried him in its own register as a citizen born in New York, and he came back to America on an Italian passport. This is the paper he had with him at Ellis Island. The register entry itself (Lascari, Nati 1912, Parte II) is online at the Antenati portal, in the same volume the town hall copied this from.

Comune di Lascari, transcription into the 1912 birth register, act of 7 September 1912; certified copy of 25 February 1924. Kept by the family; scanned in 2022.

Anything of Stefano's, Papa Petey's or Grandma Jean's: above all Stefano's alien registration card from 1940–41, which alone would settle the 1940 question. Also an Italian passport, naturalization papers, funeral or mass cards, a family Bible.

Memories

Which church Papa Petey and Grandma Jean married in, in 1933. Where the family lived around 1930. Anything anyone remembers about Stefano, or about the Lisuzzos in Albany.

Later, from the direct descendants

Papa's long-form birth certificate, Papa and Nana's marriage certificate, and Nana's birth certificate (New York City, it turns out, not Albany) can only be ordered by a child. Consulates want recently issued copies, so this waits until a route is chosen.

What we can still look for ourselves

Besides what the family may have in a drawer, these are the searches still open; each one answers a numbered question in the list above. Most are free; the paid ones are marked.

To settle the 1940 question Q1

USCIS Genealogy index search for Stefano ($30, about nine months): returns any naturalization file and any 1940–41 alien registration. If nothing comes back, a certificate of non-existence of a naturalization record is the document a consulate accepts. Also worth a look: the Lascari and Milazzo "cittadinanze" registers (acts about gaining or losing Italian citizenship), and Stefano's military service record in the Messina state archive, which would show him as an Italian subject.

The 1924 "court case", now explained Q8

Nana's account settles what happened: weeks on Ellis Island, then the midwife's affidavit and the baptismal certificate. The only thing left to find, for the curious, is the Board of Special Inquiry file itself in the National Archives, which would name the witnesses. A cheaper and more useful request: ask the Comune di Lascari for an Italian birth extract of Giuseppe Jr. from the 1912 act, and for his father's death act, in one letter.

The missing censuses Q5 Q9

The Boninas have not been found in the 1930 census: they were not yet at Garfield Place. (The Lisuzzos' 1930 page turned up under "Lisuggo".) The 1925 New York state census page for "Steve Bonnina" (image on Ancestry, which needs a subscription) would give the 1925 address, and from an address a census page can be found even when the name index fails, as the 1920 page was.

Papa Petey and Grandma Jean's 1933 marriage Q3

Not in any free index. The City Clerk's 1933 license index is on microfilm at the NYC Municipal Archives (a visit, or a paid search), and the parish register of whichever church married them is the other copy. Grandma Jean's family was in Coney Island; John and Nancy married at Our Lady of Loreto in 1931.

The Italian acts still to pull Q4 Q6 Q7

In Lascari: Giuseppe Lisuzzo's birth (about 1865 to 1867; the registers for 1864 and 1866 to 1870 do not have it, and 1865 is online nowhere, so it is a request to the comune) and Maria Stella Cirrito's death. The registers to 1910 are on FamilySearch without a name index, but the handwriting transcripts on FamilySearch make them searchable page by page; the registers from 1911 to 1947 are on the Antenati portal, which is where the 1926 marriage and the 1941 death came from. In Milazzo: Natala Trimboli's birth (about 1872) and the deaths of Giovanni and Adelaide. In Brooklyn: Natala's death certificate (she died between 1910 and 1920; the family chart says 1912).

Later: the legal side Q11

A consultation with an Italian citizenship lawyer on the two-year residence route, and on how a grandparent who died without ever being recognized is proven. How this was found

Everything on this page came from free, public sources: FamilySearch (name indexes and record images, including census pages browsed district by district when the index failed), the NYC Municipal Archives' historical vital records, the Italian State Archives' Antenati portal (which also holds Lascari's own registers from 1911 to 1947) and FamilySearch's copies of the Termini Imerese and Messina court registers, the US National Archives (1940 census catalog), and the volunteer indexes of the Italian and German genealogy groups. The law was read from the consolidated text published by an Italian consulate, the Interior Ministry's operating circular, and the Italian Embassy in Washington.

Each record was read in full and transcribed into a private working file, with its archive, record number, stable web address and a checksum of the file, so every item here can be found again. Names and dates drift between records (Bonina / Bonino / Bonbonea; Cesare / Cesar; a birth year that moves by fourteen years); the discrepancies are listed in the working file because they are what a consulate asks about.

A few records are not from archives at all: Nana's own papers, kept in the family and scanned in 2022, reached this page through Gianni's CD and Mom in October 2026, along with Gianni's charts and photographs. They are marked "kept by the family" under each image.

Dad's side of the family (the Furino and Dascole lines) has its own file and will get its own page.
