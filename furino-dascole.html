In short

Grandpa Frank Francis Furino, our grandfather
Grandma Emily Emily Dascole, our grandmother
Dad Sal, their son

Salvatore Furino (born Saviano, near Naples, 1896) landed in New York in November 1912, was still an Italian subject when the Army registered him in 1917, fought in France, and became an American citizen on 25 August 1919, sixteen days after marrying Frances Catapano. Grandpa Frank was born years later, to two Americans. The Furino line carries no Italian citizenship, under the old rules or the new.

On Grandma Emily's side the story is the reverse. Her grandfather John D'Ascoli came from the Naples countryside to Brooklyn around 1889, raised a family on Jackson Street, took out first papers before 1910, and was still an alien in 1920 and in 1930. He died at 34 Jackson Street on 25 July 1934, before Grandma Emily was born. If he never finished naturalizing, she had a grandfather who was only Italian on the day that counts, and she meets the 2025 law's test in her own right Q1.

Under Italy's 2025 law, none of this passes by descent to Dad, or to Sal and Stephanie: Dad's parents and grandparents were born in America or had become American.

It can open the two-years-of-residence route: a foreigner whose parent or grandparent was an Italian citizen by birth can naturalize after two years of legal residence in Italy. If Grandma Emily's case holds, that fits Dad through her, and Sal and Stephanie through her as a grandparent, on top of what Mom's side already gives them.

The 2025 law is before the EU Court of Justice. If it is reversed (decision expected late 2027 or 2028), the Dascole line would qualify by descent all the way down, through John, James and Grandma Emily. The Furino line would not, either way.

The 2025 law, briefly

Until March 2025 Italy recognized citizenship by descent with no limit on generations: you only had to show an unbroken line from an Italian-born ancestor, with nobody in the line having naturalized elsewhere before the next child was born. Decree-law 36/2025, converted into Law 74/2025, added article 3-bis: a person born outside Italy who holds another citizenship is treated as never having been Italian, unless an exception applies. The exception that matters for families like ours: a parent or grandparent held exclusively Italian citizenship on the day the person was born (or on the day that ancestor died, if earlier).

Italy's Constitutional Court upheld the law in March 2026. In July 2026 it asked the EU Court of Justice whether taking citizenship away retroactively is compatible with EU law (case C-816/26). Until that court rules, the law applies in full. Separately, the law cut the residence requirement for naturalization from three years to two for anyone with a parent or grandparent who "is or was a citizen by birth".

The two things a consulate or court asks for are the same under both sets of rules: civil records for every link in the chain (birth, marriage, death), and proof of whether the Italian-born ancestor ever naturalized abroad. That is what the records below are for. On this side of the family the first question is answered twice over: Salvatore Furino did naturalize, with the papers to show it, and John D'Ascoli, as far as the records go, did not.

Why this side is closed

Under the old rules an Italian who naturalized abroad lost his Italian citizenship that day, and children born afterwards were not Italian. Salvatore naturalized in August 1919; Grandpa Frank was born years later. Under the 2025 law the test is whether a parent or grandparent was exclusively Italian on the day you were born: Salvatore had been an American for years, and Frances had become one with him. So nothing passes through this line, and the records here settle it rather than open it.

One question stays for a lawyer, and only if the EU court restores the old rules: Italian courts have recognized descendants of women who lost citizenship involuntarily before 1948, as Frances did when her husband naturalized. Whether that would reach Grandpa Frank, born after she had become American, is doubtful, but it is the kind of thing a specialist should say Q9.

Still to find on this side: Salvatore's birth act in Saviano and Frances's birth and arrival, all requests rather than searches now Q4 Q5; and the family's 1920 census page, which the name index has lost Q6.

Why this side matters

Under the old rules the line John → James → Emily is unbroken if John was still Italian in December 1897, which the 1910 census ("first papers", not yet a citizen) says he was; James, born American, never had to naturalize, so he kept whatever Italian citizenship he had, and so did his daughter. Under the 2025 law, Grandma Emily needs a parent or grandparent who was exclusively Italian on the day she was born, or on the day that ancestor died, if earlier. Her father was American-born. Her grandfather John died before she was born: the question is his status on 25 July 1934, the day he died. Every record after 1900 says alien.

What hangs on it: Grandma Emily's own standing as an Italian citizen by birth, and through her, the two-year residence route for Dad (as her son) and for Sal and Stephanie (as her grandchildren). It would not give any of them citizenship by descent while the 2025 law stands.

Still to find on this side: proof that John never completed naturalization Q1, the town he came from and his own birth and marriage records Q2, and James's birth certificate Q3.

A man who had first papers in 1910 and is an alien in 1920 and 1930 is a man who let his declaration lapse; the 1900 "naturalized" is an answer given about a recent immigrant who could not yet have qualified. The free court indexes (the federal courts in Brooklyn and Manhattan; the Kings County court 1907–1924) have no naturalization for him. But an absence in indexes is not proof. Two things would settle it:

  • A USCIS Genealogy index search ($30, about nine months): it returns any naturalization file from 1906 on, and would also show the court and the town of birth from his first papers.
  • Anything the family kept: his declaration of intention, a naturalization certificate (which would cut the other way, and we would want to know), his or Caroline's alien registration card from 1940–41.

Open questions

Everything on this page that is not settled, in one list. The Q marks in the text point here. Each one says what hangs on it, what would settle it, and who can help.

  1. Q1
    Did John D'Ascoli ever complete his naturalization?

    First papers by 1910, alien in 1920 and 1930, his widow an alien in 1946; only the 1900 census says "naturalized". Grandma Emily's standing as Italian by birth under the 2025 law, and the residence route for Dad and for us through her, turn on this.

    What would settle it: A USCIS Genealogy index search ($30, about nine months). Or his papers, if anyone kept them: a declaration, a certificate, an alien registration card.

    Where did John and Caroline come from, and when were they born and married?

    Every record says only "Italy". His parents were Alfonso D'Ascoli and Rosa Pinto; hers, Felice Allocca and Giuseppina. What we need from the family

The open questions a relative might answer are marked Family can help in the list above.

Grandma Emily's memories

Where her grandfather John said he came from Q2; whether he ever "got his papers" Q1; whether her father ever had a birth certificate, or a baptismal certificate from Mount Carmel Q3; and whether she wants this looked into for herself.

Old papers

Salvatore Furino's naturalization certificate (no. 1166313, 1919) or his Army discharge; anything of John or Caroline D'Ascoli's: first papers, an alien registration card from 1940–41, a passport, funeral cards. Any of these would settle Q1 one way or the other.

Frances Catapano

What town she called home, and whether anyone has her papers or a photograph of her parents Q5.

Later, from the direct descendants

Grandma Emily's and Grandpa Frank's long-form birth certificates and their marriage certificate can only be ordered by them or their children. Consulates want recently issued copies, so this waits until a route is chosen Q8.

What we can still look for ourselves

The free searches are done; what is left is mostly letters and one paid search. Each item answers a numbered question in the list above.

To settle Grandma Emily's question Q1

USCIS Genealogy index search for John D'Ascoli ($30, about nine months): any naturalization file from 1906 on. If nothing comes back, a certificate of non-existence of a naturalization record is the document a consulate accepts. The free indexes have already been searched.

The Italian acts Q2 Q4

Letters, free or nearly so, to the Comune di Nola (John's birth, about 1851–54; his marriage to Carolina Allocca, about 1880–85) and the Comune di Saviano (Salvatore's birth, 1896; his parents' marriage). Neither town's registers after 1865 are online.

The missing Brooklyn papers Q3 Q6

James's birth certificate: a search by date and parents at the Municipal Archives, including delayed registrations. The Furinos' 1920 census: the district for 562 Metropolitan Avenue, read page by page. John and Caroline's arrival, about 1889, in the pre-Ellis Island passenger lists.

Frances Catapano Q5

Her manifest, in the Ellis Island index under Catapano and its spellings, matched by her parents' names; then her birth act from the comune.

Later: the legal side Q9

A consultation with an Italian citizenship lawyer on the two-year residence route through a grandparent who was never recognized, and on how a case like Grandma Emily's is proven. Certified copies, apostilles and translations only after a route is chosen.

How this was found

Everything on this page came from free, public sources: FamilySearch (name indexes and record images: census pages, draft cards, naturalization papers, passenger lists), the NYC Municipal Archives' historical vital records (births to 1909, marriages to 1949, deaths to 1948, searched by name and, where the name index failed, by certificate number), the US National Archives, and the volunteer indexes of the Italian and German genealogy groups. The law was read from the consolidated text published by an Italian consulate, the Interior Ministry's operating circular, and the Italian Embassy in Washington.

Each record was read in full and transcribed into a private working file, with its archive, record number, stable web address and a checksum of the file, so every item here can be found again. Names drift between records (D'Ascoli, Dascolo, Dascole, Drascole; Furino, Turino; Allocca, Allocea, Amena), and ages drift by years; the discrepancies are listed in the working file because they are what a consulate asks about.

Mom's side of the family, the Bonina and Lisuzzo lines, is on its own page.
